Selling into China under the bonded model works when seven business flows hang on the same ledger, not when you have wired up the most supplier interfaces. The conditions are concrete: from payment to signed delivery, every order writes the commerce, goods, logistics, funds, information, compliance and invoicing records under one order number; fourteen capabilities are split into deliverables that can be accepted one by one, and one party answers for the result from zone entry to signed delivery. Without that ledger the daily report has to be stitched by hand, which looks fine once and fails within a month.

## Why a Daily Report Starts With an Order Number

On the chapter divider we leave three acceptance questions: how the seven flows form one chain, which steps an order passes from checkout to signature, and whether daily operations and mega-sale peaks can both be sustained. All three come down to whether one order number pulls the records of all seven flows when something goes wrong. If it does, the daily report is a by-product; if it does not, you ask four suppliers for four spreadsheets and align them by hand.

## Seven Flows Are Not Seven Systems

The overview page puts the seven flows in one row: commerce, goods, logistics, funds, information, compliance and invoicing. They are not seven systems to procure but seven kinds of records you must keep. Commerce records orders and contracts, goods records batches and shelf life, logistics records tracking and signed delivery, funds records receipts, payments and balances, information records interfaces and order status, compliance records declarations, filings and checkpoints, and invoicing records invoices and credit notes. One order number shared by all seven kinds is the ledger.

The customs supervision code for this channel is 1210, formally bonded cross-border trade e-commerce. An order is released only after the order, payment and logistics records are matched one by one under the verification rules in Announcement No. 486 of 2018 issued by six ministries including the Ministry of Commerce. Those per-order records are exactly what the compliance flow writes each day.

What makes this structure run is seven flows on one ledger, not steps stitched together from different vendors. A stitched setup looks the same on a normal day and falls apart at the first refund or amendment: the store has cancelled the order, the payment record is still open, the parcel has already left the warehouse, and nobody holds the full answer.

![Seven flows one ledger overview with the seven business flows, fourteen modules, three explanatory cards and a three-day report extract](/images/insights/seven-flows-one-ledger-bonded-exports-china/seven-flows-14-modules-overview.webp)

Patchwork has a harder consequence: nobody owns compliance, data or invoicing as a whole. When one flow breaks, everything behind it stops, most often at remittance or clearance. That is why the first card on the overview page is dark blue and reads one entity orchestrates, with the seven flow names on the other cards.

## How Fourteen Modules Are Split by Signing Authority

The fourteen modules run from S0 to S13 and map to fourteen items in the service catalog that can be delivered and accepted separately. They are split by signing authority, not by system: each module hands over a deliverable somebody signs for. The first seven run through release and settlement, the last seven cover operations and retained records.

- S0 Diagnosis and access: the access decision and its basis, with category, tariff code and target channel laid out first.
- S1 Entity setup: business licence and customs registration; without either the system cannot open an account.
- S2 Platform store: launch and payment activation, where category qualifications are put in place.
- S3 Product filing: grading and documents; a wrong tariff code means amending every later declaration.
- S4 Bonded warehouse: a bonded ledger view with stock visible to the brand.
- S5 Clearance release: the order, payment and logistics records are matched one by one before release.
- S6 Funds settlement: statements and vouchers, with goods payments and service fees recorded apart.
- S7 Distribution: acquisition and promotion attribution, recalculated from the order number.
- S8 After-sales: returns handling; how return reasons are classified decides whether member data is clean.
- S9 Data risk control: data compliance, where cross-border transfer and retention rules are set.
- S10 Compliance audit: self-checks and logs, used to demonstrate what happened when something goes wrong.
- S11 Supply chain: order and stock views, so pre-orders do not rest on guesses.
- S12 Content review: pages and livestream scripts, with Chinese labels and claims reviewed batch by batch.
- S13 Membership and subscription: tier data taken from the same ledger.

Three cards fix how to use them. The first explains why the seven flows must be one set: in a patchwork nobody owns compliance, data or invoicing, one broken flow holds up the rest, and remittance or clearance stalls first. The second explains the fourteen modules: S0 to S13 map to service catalog deliverables, while the 9610 direct purchase channel and a second supply source are future items with slots reserved, not part of today's acceptance. The third fixes accountability: from zone entry to signed delivery there is one accountable party, the A in RACI; capabilities can be swapped, accountability does not move.

One more instruction sits under the overview page: all fourteen capabilities are itemised, with no vendor-by-vendor comparisons and no multi-party integration. Deliverables and methods follow the service catalog; what goes into the contract is those fourteen signed items, not a list of interfaces.

## Walking Through the 09-24 Row

Take the 09-24 row as something to investigate. 128 paid orders and 62400.00 yuan divide exactly into an average order value of 487.50 yuan; 121 paying buyers is seven fewer than the order count, so somebody placed more than one order that day; a 1.8% refund rate maps to the returns still open in the after-sales module.

Trace the seven flows back from that row: commerce pulls the contracts and attribution for those 128 orders, goods gives batches and shelf life, logistics gives dispatch and signed-delivery times, funds gives receipts, payments and balances, information gives order status changes and interface receipts, compliance gives per-order declarations and the three-document match, and invoicing gives invoices and credit notes. All seven share the order number, so one lookup finds everything; if an interface callback arrives late, the information row is blank first, the paid amount and average order value still agree, but service cannot say where that order is.

The mapping on the third card holds up here: with order, product and member data from one back office, paying buyers can be reconciled with membership tiers. Split across three back offices, the figure 121 needs a cross-database join, and once that join rule changes tomorrow's report stops being comparable with today's.

## Which Flow Breaks First on a Mega-Sale Day

The third question on the divider asks whether daily operations and mega-sale peaks can both be sustained. Under this structure they can, provided three things are fixed before the peak: one order number running through all seven flows, one accountable party from zone entry to signed delivery, and a report generated by the back office rather than assembled from exports. A peak loads interface callbacks and stock sync; the order number does not change, so the ledger stays the same ledger. With a manually stitched report, a peak adds workload and more chances to stitch the wrong rows.

Fix the fields before launch: date, paid order count, paid amount, average order value, paying buyers, refund rate and action entries, all seven columns read from the same tables. Then decide who signs the fourteen deliverables. The rest can be added later; slots for the 9610 direct purchase channel and a second supply source already sit in the S0 to S13 mapping, and the accountable party does not change when they are filled.

The dates, amounts and order counts on the screen are demo data, included to show how one row is built and traced back. Actual figures follow the current statements, platform exports and the requirements of the competent authority; refund rate and average order value follow the system's calculation for that day.

Seven flows on one ledger is not a piece of architecture language. On paper it is fourteen deliverables somebody signs for; on screen it is a daily report that grows one row a day, with every row traceable. Fix the order number and the accountable party first, then choose suppliers. Reverse that order and every reconciliation afterwards has to be done again.
