A China sales plan built on Mode 1210 puts more than corporate income tax on the onshore entity's desk. Four duties attach directly to this channel: VAT on service fees, corporate income tax, collection and remittance of import-stage taxes, and outward remittance compliance. The first two are the entity's own taxes. The other two are import taxes it collects and pays over for someone else, and the document checks that let money leave the country. Each duty has one voucher type, and the four voucher types have to reconcile on the same transaction.

When they do not, both ends lock up. Service-fee invoices that do not match the commission documentation fail the pre-tax deduction for corporate income tax. Remittance documents that do not match the customs declaration stop the payment. One further policy is often carried across by mistake: the pilot-zone invoice-free exemption and the 4% deemed assessment at a taxable income rate are retail export policies, and they do not apply on the import side. Draw that line first, then discuss tax planning.

## Four Duties, Four Voucher Types

Read the table row by row. Row one is VAT on modern services: the onshore entity files it, the voucher is the service-fee invoice, and the common misuse is exempting it under an export policy. Row two is corporate income tax: the onshore entity still files, the voucher is the commission documentation, and the common misuse is a deduction refused because the four voucher types are incomplete. Row three is outward remittance: the platform or the declaring enterprise handles it, the voucher is the remittance document set, and the common misuse is documents that do not correspond to the customs declaration. Row four is import-stage tax: the purchaser is the taxpayer, the enterprise collects and remits on their behalf, the voucher is the customs declaration or declaration list, and that declaration has to corroborate the remittance documents.

The first two rows decide the onshore entity's profit and loss. The last two decide whether the payment can go out and whether the import taxes can be matched. Work them as one line and tax collected for someone else becomes your own cost, while a document problem on the remittance side only surfaces at the exchange step.

Nobody needs to memorise that table. Check it once a month: revenue side against the service-fee invoice, cost side against the commission documentation, funds side against the remittance documents, goods side against the customs declaration. The four voucher types close on the same transaction, and only then is the tax paid properly.

## Service-Fee Invoices: 6% VAT Charged on Modern Services

Service fees the onshore entity charges the overseas brand owner fall under modern services, and VAT applies at 6%. This tax does not turn into an export exemption because the payer sits abroad. The service is performed in China and consumed in China, the invoice is issued in China, and VAT is paid at 6%. Whether a cross-border transaction qualifies for exemption depends on where the service is actually performed and consumed. Reading only the payer's place of registration is not enough.

A monthly service-fee contract of 10,000 US dollars converts at 7.2 yuan per dollar to 72,000 yuan; VAT is 4,320 yuan, and the invoice total is 76,320 yuan. Sign the contract price exclusive of tax, list price and tax separately on the invoice, and have the overseas parent pay the total. If the contract price is tax-inclusive, work the exclusive price back out of the 10,000 US dollars: the rate is still 6% and only the base changes. Either form works, as long as the contract and the invoice use the same basis.

The common misuse is treating a service fee as export goods and applying the pilot-zone invoice-free exemption. That exemption covers goods sold retail out of a pilot zone, and service fees are not on the list. An uninvoiced service fee leaves the revenue side without its first voucher, and the commission deduction and the remittance review both break further downstream.

## Corporate Income Tax: Whether Commission Cost Is Deductable Depends on the Voucher Chain

Estimate corporate income tax at the statutory 25%. Taxable income is revenue less deductible costs and expenses, and a commission payment needs a complete voucher chain before it can be deducted. STA Announcement No. 28 of 2018 sets out that chain plainly: the vouchers must be genuine, lawful and related to the expense; for expenditure on taxable domestic items the invoice is the pre-tax deduction voucher; and contracts, payment records and evidence of what was delivered have to be kept for inspection.

Commission documentation is a group of records: the promotion services contract, the settlement statement, the payment record and the invoice, plus withholding receipts where an individual is paid. Those records have to match the service-fee invoice on the revenue side for the same period and the same transaction. When the four voucher types do not match, the commission is not deductible, book profit rises, and corporate income tax is assessed on the difference later.

At 25%, a commission of 3,000 US dollars converted to 21,600 yuan costs 5,400 yuan of additional corporate income tax if the deduction is refused, about 750 US dollars. If the entity qualifies as a small low-profit enterprise, the effective rate is below 25% and the additional tax is recomputed at the applicable rate, while the voucher chain requirement stays as it is.

## Outward Remittance: Documents That Miss the Customs Declaration Hold the Payment

Remittance compliance is handled by the platform or the declaring enterprise, and the bank reviews the trade background: the contract, the invoice and the customs declaration must agree on amount, date and counterparty. If the operating unit, the consignee, the goods, the quantity or the amount on the declaration differs from the remittance application, the bank returns the request or asks for a written explanation. The goods have entered the zone, the payment is stuck at the bank, and supplier terms go out of order.

One payment of 15,000 US dollars corresponds to a customs declaration for 1,000 jars at 15 US dollars each. If the declaration shows 13,000 US dollars and the remittance application 15,000, the 2,000 US dollar gap is the problem: amend the declaration, split the order, or add an explanation. Until the gap is resolved, the remittance is not released. Service fees and commissions are separate lines, and folding them into the goods payment only makes the documents harder to match.

Remittance documents are the funds side of the four voucher types. They prove the payment only when they corroborate the customs declaration. The invoice proves revenue, the commission documentation proves cost, the declaration proves goods, and the remittance set proves funds. Four lines closing on one transaction is a complete set of books.

## Import-Stage Taxes: The Purchaser Is the Taxpayer, the Enterprise Collects and Remits

The taxpayer for import-stage taxes is the purchaser, meaning the consumer who places the order. Under Cai Guan Shui [2016] No. 18, the tariff on cross-border e-commerce retail imports is provisionally set at 0%, and import VAT and consumption tax are collected at 70% of the statutory amount. For goods in the 13% VAT band that carry no consumption tax, that works out to 9.1%. Platforms, logistics providers and customs brokers registered with the Customs authority collect and remit it, and the onshore entity is usually neither the taxpayer nor the bearer of that burden.

The onshore entity has to keep two roles apart: the taxpayer and the party that collects and remits. The first carries the burden, the second files and pays. Booking collected tax as your own cost distorts unit economics, and writing the consumer's tax burden into a service-fee quote inflates the price. Import-stage tax sits in the order price, not in the service-fee invoice.

The customs declaration is the voucher for this duty and the document that corroborates the remittance set. Amounts, goods and quantities on the declaration or declaration list have to match the remittance application and the order data. When they do not, both the tax base collected on behalf of purchasers and the trade background behind the payment come into question. Even where the entity does no collecting and remitting, the order and product data it supplies has to support the customs declaration and the remittance documents.

## Three Entity Routes, Different Taxpayers and Different Invoice Issuers

A wholly foreign-owned enterprise, a joint venture, and the platform acting as principal place tax and invoicing on different bodies. A WFOE issues the service-fee invoice itself and pays VAT and corporate income tax itself. In a joint venture the JV company invoices and pays, and profit distributions attract withholding tax handled under the applicable treaty and domestic law. Where the platform is the principal, it becomes the import and sales entity, the brand owner's onshore entity moves back to the service-provider position, and the invoice flow and the burden shift with it.

The route changes who issues each of the four vouchers, while the closing relationship stays the same: an invoice on the revenue side, documentation on the cost side, remittance documents on the funds side, a customs declaration on the goods side. When choosing a route, look first at who invoices, who pays tax and who collects and remits, then at whether the four vouchers can close on one entity. If the invoice issuer and the taxpayer are misaligned, the four vouchers will not match however complete they look.

Once the entity is established, the collection method is confirmed with the competent tax authority. Audit-based collection and deemed collection are two different methods of assessing corporate income tax, with different conditions and filing requirements. Neither one is the 4% deemed assessment at a taxable income rate for pilot-zone retail exports, and the two sets of rules cannot be interchanged. ## The Invoice-Free Exemption and the 4% Deemed Assessment Belong to Retail Exports

The pilot-zone invoice-free exemption comes from Cai Shui [2018] No. 103. An e-commerce export enterprise in a comprehensive pilot zone that exports goods without a valid purchase voucher may trial exemption from VAT and consumption tax when three conditions are met at the same time: registration in the pilot zone with the export information recorded on its online integrated services platform, export declaration filed through the customs office where the pilot zone is located, and goods that are not on the list of exports denied refund or exemption. Its object is goods sold retail out of China: not imports, and not service fees.

The 4% deemed assessment comes from STA Announcement No. 36 of 2019. Qualifying cross-border e-commerce retail export enterprises in a pilot zone are assessed for corporate income tax on a deemed taxable income rate, set uniformly at 4%. The 4% is a taxable income rate, and applying the statutory 25% corporate income tax rate gives an effective burden of 1% of revenue; enterprises that also meet the small low-profit criteria can stack the preferential treatment. It pairs with the invoice-free exemption, and its subject is still the retail export enterprise.

The import side follows the rules of No. 18: tariff provisionally at 0%, VAT and consumption tax at 70% of the statutory amount, and 9.1% as the result for goods in the 13% band with no consumption tax. The invoice-free exemption does not apply on the import side, and neither does the deemed assessment at a 4% taxable income rate. Customs declarations, duty-paid receipts and remittance documents are the tax evidence on that side.

Carrying an export policy across to the import side costs a retrospective adjustment. Service fees left uninvoiced put a gap in the revenue side, and the commission then fails the deduction. Applying the 4% deemed assessment to import-side profit understates corporate income tax, which is assessed later with a late-payment surcharge at audit. The cost reaches further than the shortfall: the closing relationship among the four voucher types is broken, and every step downstream has to be redone.

## One Month of Books, with the Four Vouchers Matched

Take a month of 1,000 orders at one jar of 50 US dollars each: merchandise value 50,000 US dollars, corresponding to 1,000 jars. Goods cost 15,000 US dollars at 15 US dollars a jar, and both the customs declaration and the remittance documents carry 15,000 US dollars. Service fees are 10,000 US dollars, or 72,000 yuan at 7.2 yuan per dollar, VAT at 6% is 4,320 yuan and the invoice total is 76,320 yuan. Commission is 3,000 US dollars, or 21,600 yuan. Warehousing and customs brokerage are 2,000 US dollars, or 14,400 yuan, with 864 yuan of VAT. Staff and rent are 3,000 US dollars, or 21,600 yuan.

That month's profit is 10,000 less 3,000 less 2,000 less 3,000, which is 2,000 US dollars, or 14,400 yuan; corporate income tax at 25% is 3,600 yuan, about 500 US dollars. Output VAT on service fees is 4,320 yuan. The warehousing and brokerage invoices are VAT special invoices, so the 864 yuan of input VAT offsets against it and VAT payable is 3,456 yuan, about 480 US dollars. Import-stage tax at 9.1% of the 50,000 US dollar order price including freight and insurance comes to 4,550 US dollars, is borne by the consumer, and stays off the onshore entity's profit and loss. The goods payment is collected and paid on someone else's behalf; whether it enters the entity's revenue depends on the entity route and the contract, and this example recognises service-fee revenue.

The four vouchers sit where they belong this month. The service-fee invoice is issued at a total of 76,320 yuan, the commission documentation covers 3,000 US dollars, the remittance documents 15,000 US dollars and the customs declaration 15,000 US dollars. When the four match on the same transaction, the invoice can be issued, the remittance released and the commission deducted. If any one of them is missing, complete the voucher before issuing the invoice, and do not issue first and patch afterwards.

If the commission documentation is missing on 3,000 US dollars, the deduction fails and corporate income tax comes out 750 US dollars higher. If the remittance application is 15,000 US dollars while the declaration shows only 13,000, the 2,000 US dollar gap leaves the payment standing at the bank. If the service fee is treated as invoice-free, 10,000 US dollars of revenue has no invoice, and the commission deduction and the remittance review break together. The fix for all three is the same: go back to the four voucher types, reconcile item by item, then take the next step.
