How long a bonded pilot takes from kick-off to launch lands in a conditional range of about fourteen to thirty-four weeks, not on a fixed date. Where you land depends on two things on the critical path, entity registration and the platform plus fund accounts, and on the slippage factors you control. The reading given by chapter seven, Clear Numbers, of the source material: treat time as a range, cost as a structure, and leave returns to your own model. This article covers the four pages of chapter seven in one pass: the critical path and nine phases, the range table of our controllables versus your inputs, six slippage factors and their cost, and the six milestones with the inputs they need. ## The critical path and nine phases from kick-off to launch

One marker anchors the schedule: D-Day, the launch day, is the first bonded release day and the hardest checkpoint of Phase 1. The timeline runs from today out to week thirty-four. The legend has three colors: dark navy bars for the critical path, mid-blue bars for non-critical stages, light-blue bars for recurring operations after D-Day. Nine rows run top to bottom: S0 diagnostic checks about two weeks, mid-value shown on the chart; S1 entity setup four to twelve weeks, on the critical path; S2 platform and fund accounts four to eight weeks, parallel with S1; S3 product admission and filing two to six weeks, parallel with S2; S4 first-leg freight and zone entry two to four weeks; S5 three-document clearance one to two weeks; then D-Day first release as the key checkpoint; S6 settlement on a monthly rhythm; and S7 plus, ongoing operations in steady state.

![Chapter 7 schedule chart: Kick-off to Launch critical path and nine phases gantt, nine time bars from S0 to S7+ with the D-Day checkpoint line](/images/blog/bonded-pilot-china-timeline-delay-causes/kick-off-to-launch-critical-path-nine-phases.webp)

The page is titled Kick-off to Launch: Critical Path and Nine Phases. The nine phases are not nine relay batons: S1 runs parallel with S2, and S3 runs parallel with S2 as well, so only the segments on the critical path actually form one line. This article, together with the three pages that follow on ranges, slippage and milestones, stands on its own.

Why the critical path runs through S1 and S2 is answered by the card below the chart: the entity and the funds accounts decide whether you can open the store, declare customs, and settle. Until those two land, everything downstream queues. Starting both at once anchors the whole path.

Before and after D-Day are two ledgers. Before D-Day is one-time build-out: entity, filing, zone entry, first shipment, with budget and staffing going in once. After D-Day, recurring operations begin: settlement, support, repeat purchase, ledger, on a monthly cadence. Budget the two phases separately, not in one table.

## What the fastest fourteen weeks requires, a full worked example

The source page breaks the fastest fourteen weeks into four conditions. First, entity via the platform-agency route, one of our service items, holding S1 within four weeks. Second, CoA and test reports complete, so S3 triggers no retesting. Third, the S2 store and fund accounts launched in parallel immediately. Fourth, port preference and volumes set early, because staggered documents push the schedule toward the cap. All four are in your hands, and we can demo the modeling live on your conditions.

Walk the fourteen weeks with one set of demo conditions. S0 starts with two weeks. S1 lands in four weeks via the agency route. S2 runs out its eight-week cap in parallel without adding critical-path time. S3 absorbs its six weeks alongside S2. S4 reads two of its two-to-four weeks, provided volumes are set early and the first leg does not miss its window. S5 reads one of its one-to-two weeks. The critical path adds two weeks plus four weeks plus four weeks plus two weeks, about twelve to fourteen weeks, at the short end of the range. In reverse, entity via WFOE registration pushes S1 to the twelve-week cap; documents arriving in batches leave stages waiting in series; a late port preference needs two contingency plans; late volumes make first-leg freight miss the schedule window. Stacked together, the schedule slides toward the thirty-four-week end. Both ends are on the table so expectations can be set accurately.

The terms here: the range is conditional; the thirty-four-week cap corresponds to WFOE registration plus staggered documents; apart from S0 shown at mid-value for the demo, durations on the chart are ranges, not mid-values.

## Six slippage factors and their cost

Six slippage factors decide which end of the range you land on; bar length shows relative impact on a conditional basis. Ownership follows the three-way split: dark navy is our side, mid-blue is you provide, ink is in parallel with us.

| Owner | Factor | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You provide | CoA and test readiness | Retest: S3 slips two to six weeks |
| You provide | Document turnaround | List late: S0 start slips about two weeks |
| You provide | SKU and classification load | More items: longer filing |
| Our side | Basis verification | Rework: absorbed in stage |
| In parallel | First-leg mode | Mode choice affects S4 |
| You provide | Entity path (four to twelve weeks) | Late call: path slips one for one |

| Delay case | Slip cost | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SKU list two weeks late | slips about two weeks | S0 start slips |
| Missing CoA retested | slips two to six weeks | S3 slips |
| Entity path decision late | slips one for one | critical path |
| Port processing | standard handling time | caps the S5 schedule |
| Volumes late | no fixed weeks | affects S4 |

The first three factors are in your hands: CoA readiness, document turnaround, and SKU and classification load decide which end of the range you land on. What we execute is retest planning, document checklists, list comparison, and gap-filling; you provide originals and authorizations.

## Six milestones and the inputs we need from you

M1 through M6 are jointly confirmed delivery nodes tied to the payment cadence. Nodes are anchored to their stages; S3 product admission and filing runs in parallel with S2 and gets no separate node. The six nodes in order:

- M1, S0 done: admission call and tier sign-off, input is the SKU list and CoA.
- M2, S1 done: entity and customs registered, input is the entity path decision.
- M3, S2 done: store and accounts live, test orders placed, input is the claims strategy.
- M4, S4 done: zone entry and ledger filed, input is stocking volumes.
- M5, S5 done: D-Day first release, input is port preference.
- M6, S6 done: first settlement and reconciliation done, payment on deliverables.

| Input | Used for | If missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SKU list with specs | classification filing | S0 start slips |
| Same-batch CoA | admission call | S3 slips |
| Test reports | gap-filling endorsement | no fixed weeks |
| Volume stocking plan | first-leg scheduling | S4 schedule slips |
| Entity path decision | S1 start | critical path slips |
| Port preference | sets the target port | no fixed weeks |
| Claims strategy | content approval | no fixed weeks |

Remember three latest timings for your inputs: the SKU list and CoA before S0 starts, volumes before S4 scheduling, and the entity path before S1 starts. The six nodes are the shared baseline for the monthly review: each node maps to one deliverable, verifiable item by item; jointly confirmed, the nodes enter the payment cadence; node status updates with the monthly ledger, from the same source as the curve above.
