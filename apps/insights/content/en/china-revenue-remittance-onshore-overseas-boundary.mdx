Yes, it can be remitted, but on the onshore entity's own payment path: once the platform settles, the Chinese entity handles the outward payment against the service agreement and valid documents. A single payment above the equivalent of 50,000 US dollars needs a tax filing first. The cadence follows the platform settlement, not a separate monthly schedule set by your overseas headquarters. Overseas promoter pay is not on this path: your global pool pays it, the onshore entity neither calculates nor disburses it, and their personal data never enters the onshore system.

Separate the two systems first. The onshore side is your Chinese settlement entity, responsible for onshore promoter commissions, outward payment documents and compliance records. The overseas side is your own setup, responsible for overseas promoter pay rules, the global pool and their personal data. The line between them recognises only the onshore promotion services that have been agreed: signing, settlement, tax and records sit inside it; overseas calculation, disbursement, data and the global pool sit outside it. Put that line in the agreement instead of leaving it to each party's assumptions.

## What the onshore entity needs before it remits

Four things matter before the payment goes out. Cadence follows the platform settlement: whatever cycle the platform settles on is the cycle the outward payment follows. Settlement can be multi-currency, but the currency, amount and frequency of every payment must match the contract and the documents. Amount and frequency matching the documents is the first column a bank checks. Avoid flagged abnormal flows: amounts that swing sharply in a short period, or a frequency that does not fit the volume of business, leave the explaining to you.

Three jobs sit with the onshore entity: settling commissions to onshore promoters, assembling the outward payment documents, and keeping the compliance records complete. Commissions are signed, paid and taxed onshore, with statements and withholding vouchers archived period by period. The documents are the contract, the invoice and the tax filing reference number, the items a bank checks one by one. The compliance records tie the first two together so the same batch of business can be traced afterwards. All three sit under the onshore entity, so the responsibility sits in one place too.

## Why the onshore entity does not pay overseas promoters

Under this arrangement overseas promoter pay comes out of your global pool. The onshore entity neither sets the calculation basis nor handles the disbursement, and their personal data stays in the overseas system, which the onshore side neither receives nor needs. Article 38 of the Personal Information Protection Law governs cases where personal information genuinely has to be provided outside the People's Republic of China, and requires one of the routes: a security assessment, protection certification or a standard contract. Article 39 also requires telling the overseas recipient and obtaining separate consent. When the data never entered the onshore system, that set of conditions usually has no trigger; applicability turns on the actual data flow, so if an overseas promoter list is later sent back into China, the cross-border provision has to be reassessed, with an impact assessment under Article 55 and records kept.

Three items stay with you: the overseas pay rules, the global pool and the overseas promoters' data. You set the rules, you fund and direct the pool, and storage and access to the data stay in your own systems. The onshore entity does none of these for you, and it cannot recreate the original trail belonging to the overseas system afterwards. Handing those three to the onshore side looks like saving one handover, but it merges two sets of terms into one and makes the boundary harder to explain.

## Which clause of the contract carries the boundary

The boundary cannot stay a verbal understanding. Write it into the scope clause of the service agreement at signing: onshore services cover only onshore promoter commissions, outward payment documents and compliance records; overseas calculation, disbursement and the related data are excluded, and neither the rules nor the data enter the onshore system. Inside the line are signing, settlement, tax and records; outside it are overseas calculation, disbursement, data and the global pool. Set both lists down once at signing so nobody has to guess later. Anything outside the service scope has no matching documents, tax treatment or records, and the onshore entity has nothing to show the bank for it.

The same clause should also settle overseas commission payment: overseas promoters are paid from the global pool, the onshore entity neither calculates nor disburses their pay, and their personal data does not enter the onshore system. The only commission the onshore entity handles is the recognised onshore promoter commission, which gets its own ledger line in the agreement so it never shares a statement with overseas pay. The note at the bottom of the material is worth copying into an annex: the onshore side handles only onshore commissions and outward payment documents, and takes no part in overseas disbursement.

## One technical service fee, from platform settlement to bank payment

Take a batch of supplement orders shipped from a bonded warehouse. The platform settles the batch at quarter end, and under the service agreement the onshore settlement entity owes its overseas parent a technical support service fee. One contract, two payments.

First, fix what the money is: service trade income, not payment for goods and not overseas promoter pay, with the contract, invoice and settlement statement all pointing to the same service. Under Article 1 of Announcement No. 40 of 2013 of the State Taxation Administration and the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (amended by STA Announcement No. 31 of 2018), a domestic institution paying more than the equivalent of 50,000 US dollars (not including 50,000 US dollars) for a single service trade payment overseas must file with the competent tax authority in its locality. Where one contract requires several outward payments, Article 1 of Supplementary Announcement No. 19 of 2021 requires a filing only before the first payment; once filed, the bank payment is made against the filing form number and verification code. Article 12 of the Foreign Exchange Administration Regulations (State Council Decree No. 532) requires current account foreign exchange receipts and payments to have a genuine and lawful transaction basis, and Article 14 requires payment in own foreign exchange or purchased foreign exchange against valid documents. The bank checks the documents on that basis.

The second payment needs no repeat filing, but its amount, currency and frequency still have to match the same contract and documents. The onshore entity files both filing numbers, bank receipts and compliance records under the same batch of business. With multi-currency settlement, record each payment in its actual currency instead of folding two into one loose figure. That month's overseas promoter pay is handled separately by the global pool, and no promoter names or amounts appear in the onshore ledger; the onshore side keeps only its own onshore commission statements and withholding vouchers.

The usual sticking point is the ledger line: put both kinds of money under one account and the bank asks why two payments of different character share a row. Once overseas promoter pay is mixed into onshore service fees, the contract, documents and data flow all stop matching at once, and when the bank asks who was paid and on what rule, the onshore entity has no account of its own to give.

## Where the boundary shows up first when it breaks

If you currently send an overseas promoter list back into China for a consolidated report, or have the onshore entity calculate overseas pay for you, the boundary is already broken. The first pulls the cross-border personal information conditions back into play; the second leaves the scope clause out of line with what the business actually does. The fix is straightforward: draw the real data flow once, align the scope clause with the ledger lines, and only then talk about payment cadence.

Do not expect a bank receipt to repair the boundary either. Whether the contract says who calculates, who pays and who keeps the data decides more about a smooth remittance than the receipt does: the bank checks documents and the transaction basis, the regulator asks about the stated basis and the trail, and both lead back to the moment of signing.
